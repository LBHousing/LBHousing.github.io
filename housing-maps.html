How We Got Here

1930s–1940s · Redlining
The Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) graded Long Beach neighborhoods by perceived "lending risk" — but race was the dominant factor. The map above shows the results: communities of color were coded "Hazardous" (red), cutting them off from mortgage lending and trapping residents in deteriorating housing.
  • Banks refused to lend in red-graded areas — no mortgages, no home equity, no wealth building
  • The FHA adopted similar maps, amplifying the damage across federal housing programs
  • HOLC appraisers explicitly cited the presence of Black, Latino, and Asian residents as risk factors
  • Long Beach's West Side, Central, and North Long Beach were among the hardest hit
1940s–1960s · Racial Covenants
Property deeds across Long Beach included legal restrictions preventing sale to Black, Latino, and Asian residents. Even after the Supreme Court struck down enforcement in 1948, the covenants remained on deeds and the segregation patterns they created were already locked in.
  • Covenants restricted where people of color could buy, reinforcing redlined boundaries
  • White neighborhoods received FHA-backed mortgages; redlined neighborhoods did not
  • The wealth gap from denied homeownership compounds across generations
1960s–1990s · The Disinvestment Cycle
Without access to credit, homeownership, or public investment, redlined neighborhoods entered decades of decline. Property values dropped, maintenance was deferred, and housing stock aged without reinvestment. Landlords extracted rent while investing as little as possible.
  • Fair Housing Act of 1968 outlawed redlining — but the damage was already done
  • Neighborhoods remained segregated by income and race through inertia and ongoing discrimination
  • Aging buildings meant rising code violations, health hazards, and unsafe living conditions
2000s–Present · The Crisis Today
These same neighborhoods now have the highest rates of code violations, the most eviction filings, and the greatest concentration of cost-burdened renters. Corporate entities are buying up properties in these areas, and the tenants — disproportionately BIPOC — face displacement.
  • 53.1% of Long Beach renters are cost burdened — spending 30%+ of income on housing
  • 24.9% of all dwelling units are now owned by LLCs, corporations, and partnerships
  • The Block the Cuts and Build Long Beach Ordinance aims to reinvest in these communities
  • 90 years later, the map of housing distress matches the 1939 redlining map
The pattern is not a coincidence. When you overlay today's code violation data, eviction heat maps, and demographic data, the geography matches the HOLC redlining map above. The disinvestment was deliberate. The consequences are ongoing. The data on this page connects the dots — from the red lines drawn in the 1930s to the eviction notices served today.

Code Violations

Where the buildings are failing.

What the Violations Data Shows

Renters and their families deserve to live in safe, healthy homes. Housing violations jeopardize that right — they represent conditions that affect health and safety as determined by law. As you explore the dashboard above, the systemic patterns become clear.

Many of the same areas with high numbers of code violations coincide with historically disinvested parts of Long Beach that were once redlined or contained racial covenants. The buildings in these neighborhoods are older, have received less investment, and are more likely to be owned by entities focused on extracting rent rather than maintaining quality housing.

For renters: If you are living with code violations — mold, pests, broken plumbing, or other dangerous conditions — you have the right to report them. Retaliation by landlords for reporting violations is illegal under California law.

Evictions

Where displacement concentrates.

The Eviction-Violation Connection

Evictions and housing violations are two sides of the same coin. Tenants who report violations often face retaliatory eviction filings — even though retaliation is illegal. Meanwhile, tenants living in unsafe conditions may fall behind on rent due to related expenses (medical costs from mold, replacing broken appliances, temporary housing during repairs), putting them at risk of eviction for nonpayment.

The heat map above shows how eviction filings cluster in specific neighborhoods. When you compare this to the violations dashboard, the overlap is striking: the neighborhoods with the most code violations are the same ones with the most evictions.

The cycle: Disinvestment → deteriorating conditions → violations → landlord retaliation or rent increases → eviction → displacement. This cycle repeats block by block, building by building, across the same communities that were redlined a century ago.

Housing Justice is Racial Justice

The data makes the connection undeniable. Long Beach's Black and African American community has faced a housing struggle rooted in decades of discriminatory policy. Latino families face some of the highest rent burden rates in the city. Southeast Asian communities — particularly Cambodian refugees who rebuilt their lives in Long Beach — are concentrated in areas with high violation counts and aging housing stock.

These patterns are not accidents of the market. They are the direct, traceable legacy of policies that determined where people of color could live, whether they could own property, and how much investment their neighborhoods would receive. Understanding this history is essential to building policies that repair the damage rather than perpetuate it.

The data tools on this page exist for a reason: to make the invisible visible. When you can see the violations, map the evictions, and overlay the demographics, the pattern becomes impossible to ignore — and impossible to dismiss.
