The stakes

A home is more than shelter. It is the single strongest predictor of a person's health.

When rent takes more than half a paycheck, families cut back on food, medicine, and care. When a child under five is evicted, the disruption registers as an Adverse Childhood Experience — trauma that shapes physical and mental health for life. When an apartment is left to mold and disrepair, asthma and illness follow it home.

In Long Beach, housing instability is a primary driver of homelessness — and homelessness is a public-health emergency. To improve health here, you have to start with the house.

Rents here rise faster than incomes. Homeownership is out of reach for working families, recent graduates, and young professionals. Tenant protections remain fragmented and unevenly enforced — while corporate landlords and speculative investors consolidate the housing stock. The pressure lands hardest on those with the least cushion.

1939 · The lines

The government drew the map.

Federal appraisers graded Long Beach neighborhoods by "mortgage risk." Race was the deciding factor. Communities of color were colored red — "hazardous" — cut off from home lending, boxed in by racially restrictive covenants, and pushed toward the most polluted, industrial corners of the city.

Zoom in · The redlined core

Take the heart of the city.

These central blocks were graded D — the lowest rank, reserved almost entirely for where Black, Latino, and Asian families lived. For decades, the capital that builds and maintains housing was steered away from here on purpose.

Today · The same blocks

Citywide · Rent burden

It isn't one neighborhood.

Pull back, and the whole map still matches. Ninety years later, the 1939 grades predict today's rent burden across Long Beach — disinvestment drawn once, paid for ever since.

The cost · Severe burden

Half a paycheck, just to stay.

In the hardest-hit tracts, a quarter of renters pay more than half their income in rent — one emergency away from eviction, and the daily stress that comes with it. This is where housing becomes a health condition.

Who lives here

The crisis doesn't fall evenly.

Redlining and racially restrictive covenants denied families of color the chance to build generational wealth, and concentrated them in the most polluted corners of the city. The damage compounds — today the burden lands hardest on the same communities drawn out of opportunity in 1939.

What's hollowing out the housing

The homes are still standing. But three forces — speculation, neglect, and eviction — keep pulling them out from under the people who live there. We built the data to prove it.

01

Speculation

Homes bought to extract, not to house

Across Long Beach, individually owned homes are being absorbed into LLCs, corporations, and partnerships — owners who answer to investors, not neighbors. And the buildings they let slide show up in the public record: nearly a third of all code violations trace to corporate and institutional owners.

Neglect

When maintenance is deferred, people get sick

Mold. No heat. Vermin. Unsafe wiring. Since 2008 the city has logged 142,233 code-enforcement cases — and these aren't paperwork problems. They're the conditions that trigger asthma attacks and send children to the emergency room. In a redlined neighborhood, housing disrepair is a health diagnosis.

03

Eviction

And when all else fails, displacement

The pandemic moratorium showed what's possible — evictions fell to a fraction overnight. But as protections lapsed, the filings came roaring back. And a new tactic emerged: the "substantial remodel" loophole, which lets a landlord clear a building under cover of a superficial renovation. LiBRE found Long Beach outpacing all of Los Angeles on these no-fault removals.

The response

Fear keeps tenants silent. LiBRE turns isolated fear into collective power.

Long Beach Residents Empowered (LiBRE) is a grassroots, tenant-led nonprofit. For ten years it has advanced housing justice through renter protections, tenant organizing, and community economic development — guided by the people who live the crisis.

Fear of landlord retaliation keeps many tenants carrying the burden alone. LiBRE's innovation is simple and hard to copy: when neighbors organize together — and learn their rights — they can act where no individual dared.

The people

Behind every data point is a neighbor who decided not to face it alone.

Fer — when the carpet was the illness

Mayra "Fer" Fernanda Garcia Cortes shared an apartment with her mother and her non-verbal, disabled brother. The unit was infested, and the carpet was making them sick — her brother suffering, Fer unable to breathe through the asthma and the toxins. When Berro Property Management moved to evict the family, LiBRE and the tenants union fought it off — and won the carpet torn out and real flooring put in.

Fer didn't just keep her home. In November 2019 she became a LiBRE organizer, turning her family's fight into power for her neighbors.

Vicky — from eviction to tenant leader

Vicky received a 60-day eviction notice under the "substantial remodel" loophole — for a renovation the landlord admitted would take 80 days. The eviction left her with PTSD. But through LiBRE she had access to legal help. She sued her landlord in small-claims court for skipping the legal process — and won.

$10,489 won back

Today, Vicky is a tenant leader with LiBRE, helping lead the charge toward green social housing. And advocacy by the Housing Justice Coalition means Long Beach landlords must now pull permits before evicting for a "substantial remodel" — closing the loophole a little further.

Another future is possible

One pillar stops the bleeding. The other heals the wound.

LiBRE's 2025 report, Confronting the Long Beach Housing Crisis, lays out a two-part plan: protect tenants now, and build a permanently affordable, non-speculative housing future.

01

Protect tenants now

Enforce renter protections, fund the right to counsel, and organize buildings so no tenant faces displacement alone.

02

Build social housing

Community land trusts, social housing, and gardens on land the city already owns — permanently affordable, off the speculative market.

Turned into homes and gardens, that land could house the people displacement is pushing out — and grow food in the very neighborhoods the 1939 map left behind. With the 2026 World Cup and 2028 Olympics bringing new speculative pressure to Long Beach, the window to build that future is now.

A model any city can use

The organizing is open-source. So is the evidence.

Tenant-led Neighborhood Councils, Leadership Academies, and a coalition structure can take root in any renter-majority city — LiBRE has already helped replicate AB 1482 enforcement elsewhere.

And every map and dataset in this story — ownership, code violations, evictions, census burden, redlining, and vacant public land — was built from free public records and published openly. The innovation isn't just the organizing; it's giving tenants the same spatial intelligence that landlords and investors already have.
